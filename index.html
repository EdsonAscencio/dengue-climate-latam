The project: SALURBAL-Climate

Climate change and urban health in Latin America

SALURBAL-Climate is a five-year project (2023–2028) that produces evidence on how climate change affects health in the cities of Latin America. It builds on the data, the team and the partnerships of the SALURBAL project (Salud Urbana en América Latina), which since 2017 has studied how urban environments and policies shape health in the region.

  • 371cities of 100,000 inhabitants or more
  • 11countries
  • over 120peer-reviewed publications

What the project studies

  • Extreme temperatures
  • Droughts
  • Floods
  • Air pollution

Their effects on health and health inequities, the city and neighbourhood factors that worsen or soften them, and the health effects of urban adaptation and mitigation.

Four aims

  1. Generate locally relevant evidence on climate change health and equity impacts.

  2. Document health and equity impacts of climate mitigation and adaptation strategies.

  3. Support field building and capacity strengthening in policy-relevant research.

  4. Support policy action and advocacy for responding to climate change health impacts.

Co-led by the Drexel Urban Health Collaborative with the Universidad de los Andes (Bogotá), with partner institutions across the region. Funded by the Wellcome Trust.

Dengue and climate: Why this study

  • >14 millionreported dengue cases worldwide in 2024
  • >90%of them in the Americas
  • 82%of people in Latin America and the Caribbean live in urban areas (2025)

Dengue is transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes that live alongside people in cities. Temperature and rainfall shape how fast the mosquito and the virus develop, so their imprint on reported cases arrives weeks later. This study measures that imprint with one method across the cities of the region.

weeks

  1. A warm week
  2. Mosquitoes develop
  3. The virus incubates in the mosquito
  4. A person is bitten and falls ill
  5. The case is reported

What we found: Main results so far

Temperature

More dengue after warmer weeks, up to about 23.7 °C

Compare a week at

Weeks that followed 11 weeks at 24 °C had 3.77 times the dengue cases of weeks that followed 11 weeks at 18 °C, in the same city (95% interval 1.43–9.95).

The comparison is made within each city over time; it does not describe a typical city.

City profiles

Not the same in every city

Cities were grouped by climate and geography, without using dengue data. The rise of dengue with temperature was steeper in Highland and dry cities than in Subtropical seasonal cities; part of that difference lies between countries.

What comes next

A paper with the full methods and results is being prepared with the SALURBAL-Climate team. Knowing at which temperatures and after how many weeks dengue rises may help cities time prevention and early warning.
